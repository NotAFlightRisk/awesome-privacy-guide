<Short>
For most everyday browsing, probably not. A VPN changes your IP address, and it hides which sites you visit from your internet provider and whoever runs the Wi-Fi. But the VPN company can then see what your provider used to, so it's only worth it when you trust the VPN more than [your provider](#hide-from-your-provider) or [the network you're on](#untrusted-networks), or when you need to [get round a block](#get-round-blocks). It does nothing about [cookies, accounts or fingerprinting](#what-it-doesnt-do).
</Short>

## What a VPN hides, and from whom

A <Term slug="vpn">VPN</Term> routes your internet traffic through an encrypted tunnel to the VPN's server, and it goes out to the internet from there, so sites see the VPN's IP address instead of yours.[^eff] Your internet provider and whoever runs the Wi-Fi only see the tunnel. The VPN company sees where your traffic goes instead.[^eff]

HTTPS already hides a lot without one. About 95% of the pages Chrome loads use it,[^google-https] and on those, people along the way can't see which pages you read, your logins or your messages. What they can usually still see is the names of the sites and when you visit them.[^eff] The VPN company gets that same view, plus anything that isn't encrypted.

## What it doesn't do

A VPN isn't a tool for anonymity. Sites and apps can still recognise you through cookies, tracking pixels, the accounts you're signed in to and <Term slug="browser-fingerprinting">fingerprinting</Term>, and an app with location access can still use your phone's GPS.[^eff] The [browser privacy guide](/guides/browser-privacy/) deals with the browser side. EFF says VPN adverts badly oversell them as the answer to malware, government surveillance and online tracking, and that what they're actually good for is routing your connection through a different network.[^eff]

## When it's worth it

<Tip id="hide-from-your-provider" priority="optional" effort={15} title="Hiding your browsing from your internet provider">
Your provider can usually see the names of the sites you visit. Whether that matters depends on where you live.

<Region place="us">
In 2021, the FTC looked at six providers covering about 98% of US mobile internet, and found several of them combining web browsing data with other data to target ads, and putting customers into sensitive categories like race and sexual orientation.[^ftc-isp]
</Region>

<Region place="uk">
Under the Investigatory Powers Act, the Secretary of State can order providers to keep "internet connection records", which show which services you connected to, for up to 12 months.[^ipa-87][^ipa-62]
</Region>

With a VPN, your provider sees the tunnel and the VPN company sees the sites, which is only an improvement if you trust the VPN more. VPN companies get legal requests too, and EFF notes that many keep as much as internet providers do.[^eff]
</Tip>

<Tip id="untrusted-networks" priority="optional" effort={5} title="On a network you don't trust">
Public Wi-Fi is safer than VPN adverts make out. The FTC says that because encryption is so widely used now, public Wi-Fi is usually safe.[^ftc-wifi] People in range of it could still see the names of the sites you visit, just as your provider can at home. EFF suggests a VPN when you don't know who runs the network, as long as it's a VPN you trust.[^eff]
</Tip>

<Tip id="get-round-blocks" priority="optional" effort={5} title="Getting round a block">
A VPN makes it look as if you're connecting from wherever its server is, so it can get you past some blocks, like a school network that blocks sites, or some countries' restrictions. It won't get past all of them. Some countries ban VPNs, so keep up with the rules where you are before you rely on one, and streaming services are blocking more and more VPNs.[^eff]
</Tip>

## When Tor is the better tool

If a different network isn't enough and you need more anonymity, <Term slug="tor">Tor</Term> is the better tool. The VPN company can see your traffic, but Tor sends it through several servers, and no single one of them can see your browsing.[^eff] It's slower, and some sites block it or break in it. Signing in to an account in it still tells that site who you are.

## Picking one

<Tip id="pick-on-evidence" priority="essential" effort={30} title="Pick on what you can check, not what it promises">
A VPN sits where it can see all your traffic, and EFF warns that a dodgy one might collect it on purpose.[^eff] Plenty of VPNs say they keep no logs, but EFF says that isn't a guarantee. It suggests checking whether the company has been caught misleading its customers before, and looking for published independent audits, though nothing stops a company changing its ways after one.[^eff] It also matters where the company does business, since those countries' laws decide when it has to hand over data (<Term slug="jurisdiction">jurisdiction</Term>).[^eff]

If you pay by card, the VPN knows who you are. EFF suggests a gift card or a disposable card number instead,[^eff] and <App name="Mullvad" /> takes cash. The VPN still sees your IP address when you connect, though.[^eff]

Be wary of free VPN apps. EFF says some free VPNs sell your data, and that being in an app store doesn't make one safe.[^eff] A 2016 study of 283 Android apps that use the phone's VPN feature found that 75% included third-party tracking code, VirusTotal flagged over 38% for possible malware, and 18% didn't encrypt the traffic at all.[^csiro] That's old, but it shows what can hide behind a free download.

## Settings that matter

<Tip id="kill-switch-and-leaks" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Turn on the kill switch, then check for leaks">
If the VPN drops out for a moment, your device can carry on over your normal connection until it reconnects, unless the VPN's <Term slug="kill-switch">kill switch</Term> is on. Look for it in the app's settings.

Leaks happen. In that same 2016 study, 84% of the apps didn't send IPv6 traffic through the tunnel, and 66% didn't send DNS lookups through it.[^csiro] With the VPN on, open <App name="IP Leak Test" /> and check that the IP address and location it shows are the VPN's, not yours, including in its <Term slug="webrtc-leak">WebRTC</Term> check. If the DNS part shows your own internet provider, that's a <Term slug="dns-leak">DNS leak</Term>.
</Tip>
