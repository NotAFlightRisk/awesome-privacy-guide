<Short>
Pick a password manager from the list and give it a [long passphrase](#master-passphrase) you've never used anywhere else. Move in what your browser has saved, then [delete the export file](#delete-the-export) and [clear out the browser's copy](#clear-the-browser-copy). [Change your email password first](#email-first), because it can reset everything else, and change the rest as you log in to things. If you're coming from LastPass, [the passwords in it need changing too](#change-what-was-in-lastpass).
</Short>

## Pick one

There are two kinds, and the difference is who looks after your vault. <App name="Bitwarden" /> and <App name="ProtonPass" /> sync it through their own servers, but it's encrypted on your device before it leaves, so the company can't read it.[^bw-safari][^pp-import] That's what <Term slug="zero-knowledge">zero-knowledge</Term> means. <App name="KeePass" /> keeps your vault in a file on your own devices, and syncing and backing up that file is your job. If you're not sure, a synced one is the easier start, since a file you forget to back up is a vault you can lose.

If your browser already saves your passwords, you're not doing anything wrong. The NCSC says that's safe on your own devices. The main reason to move is that a separate manager works across a mix of browsers and phones, rather than tying everything to one of them.[^ncsc-pm]

## Set it up properly

<Tip id="master-passphrase" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Make the master password a long passphrase of random words">
This one password protects all the others. If a company's copy of your encrypted vault is ever stolen, whoever has it can try guesses offline for as long as they like, and that's how some LastPass customers lost money years after its breach.[^krebs]

Length does more than symbols. Rules like "add a capital, a number and a symbol" mostly get people to `Password1!`, and attackers know to try that.[^nist] EFF suggests six words picked at random from its list of 7,776, and every word you add makes it 7,776 times harder to guess.[^eff] The words have to be random rather than ones you'd choose, and EFF's page explains how to pick them with dice.

Don't use it for anything else. Writing it on paper and keeping it at home is fine, because someone would need to be in your house to read it. The NCSC says that can be done safely, as long as you think about who else can get to where you keep it.[^ncsc-pm]
</Tip>

<Tip id="lock-the-manager" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Turn on two-step login for the manager, and print the recovery code">
The manager's own account needs <Term slug="2fa">two-step login</Term> more than any other, since it holds the rest. Turning it on gives you a recovery code. On Bitwarden, losing both that code and the phone you use for two-step login can lock you out of your vault, so print the code and keep it with your master password.[^bw-recovery] Don't keep it only in the vault, because that's the one place you can't reach when you need it.
</Tip>

## Move your passwords in

The import itself takes a few minutes. The bit most people skip is the clean-up afterwards, since the export is a plain text file and your browser still has its own copy. The steps here follow each company's help pages as they were in November 2026.

<Tip id="move-without-a-file" priority="optional" effort={2} title="On an iPhone, move them across without a file">
Since iOS 26, Apple's Passwords app can hand your passwords and passkeys straight to another manager, so there's no file to clean up. It only works when the new manager supports it, which Bitwarden does.[^bw-safari]

<Steps>
1. Open the Passwords app and tap the options button.
2. Tap <Path>Export Data to Another App</Path>, tick what you want to move, and tap Continue.
3. Pick your new manager, then confirm in its app.
</Steps>
</Tip>

<Tip id="export-and-import" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Export your browser's passwords, then import them">
Every major browser can save its passwords to a CSV file, and Bitwarden, ProtonPass and KeePassXC (a KeePass app) can all import one.[^bw-chrome][^pp-import][^kpxc] Only import it once, because Bitwarden doesn't check for duplicates.[^bw-safari]

<Platforms>
<Platform name="chrome">
Open <Path>More --> Passwords and autofill --> Google Password Manager --> Settings</Path>, then select Download file next to Export passwords.[^chrome] Brave and Opera work the same way, and Edge keeps its passwords at `edge://wallet/passwords`.[^bw-chrome]
</Platform>
<Platform name="firefox">
Type `about:logins` in the address bar, open the menu at the top right and pick <Path>Export Passwords</Path>.[^bw-firefox]
</Platform>
<Platform name="macos">
Open the Passwords app and choose <Path>File --> Export All Passwords to File</Path>. Wi-Fi passwords and Sign in with Apple accounts don't come with it.[^apple-export]
</Platform>
<Platform name="ios">
If you can't use the direct move above, open <Path>Settings --> Apps --> Safari</Path>, tap Export under History and Website Data, choose Passwords and save it to Downloads.[^bw-safari]
</Platform>
</Platforms>

Then use your manager's import option and pick the file. In Bitwarden's web app it's <Path>Tools --> Import</Path>.[^bw-safari]
</Tip>

<Tip id="delete-the-export" priority="essential" effort={1} title="Delete the export file once it's imported">
The file isn't encrypted, so anyone who gets hold of it can read every password in it.[^apple-export] Delete it as soon as the import's worked, and empty the bin. An export from an iPhone gets saved to iCloud Drive, so delete it from there.[^bw-safari]
</Tip>

<Tip id="clear-the-browser-copy" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Turn off your browser's password saving, and clear out what it kept">
Otherwise you'll have two copies. You'll change a password in your manager, the old one will still be in the browser, and the browser will keep offering to save new ones.

<Platforms>
<Platform name="chrome">
In <Path>Google Password Manager --> Settings</Path>, turn off Offer to save passwords and passkeys, and Sign in automatically.[^bw-disable] Delete all Google Password Manager data clears out the rest, but it deletes your passkeys too.[^chrome] If you've saved any there, add new passkeys from your manager on those sites first.
</Platform>
<Platform name="firefox">
In <Path>Settings --> Passwords and autofill</Path>, untick the options that are already ticked, then remove your saved passwords from `about:logins`.[^bw-disable]
</Platform>
<Platform name="macos">
In <Path>Safari --> Settings --> AutoFill</Path>, untick the options, then delete the passwords you've moved from the Passwords app.[^bw-disable]
</Platform>
</Platforms>
</Tip>

<Tip id="phone-autofill" priority="essential" effort={3} title="Make it your phone's autofill">
Your phone won't fill passwords in from the new manager until you tell it to.

<Platforms>
<Platform name="ios">
Open <Path>Settings --> General --> AutoFill & Passwords</Path> and turn on your new manager.[^bw-ios]
</Platform>
<Platform name="android">
In Bitwarden, open <Path>Settings --> Autofill --> Autofill services</Path>, and when Android asks for your preferred service, choose Bitwarden.[^bw-android] Other managers have a similar option in their settings.
</Platform>
</Platforms>
</Tip>

## Change the passwords that matter first

You don't need to change a hundred passwords tonight. Change the few that can unlock the rest, then do the others as you log in to things.

<Tip id="email-first" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Change your email password first">
Every "forgot my password" link goes to your email, so whoever gets into it can reset your other accounts. Let your manager make a new random password for it, and turn on two-step login while you're there. After that, do your bank, anywhere your card is saved, and your social media.[^ncsc-email]
</Tip>

<Tip id="reused-passwords" priority="optional" effort={15} title="Find the passwords you've used more than once">
If you've used one password on several sites, a leak from any of them opens the others.[^ncsc-email] On Bitwarden, the data breach report is free, but the reused and weak password reports need the paid plan.[^bw-reports] If you're leaving Chrome, its own check finds breached and weak passwords, so it's worth running before you clear it out.[^chrome] Change what they flag as you go, starting with anything that's turned up in a breach.
</Tip>

## Let it spot fake sites for you

<Tip id="autofill-alarm" priority="essential" effort={1} title="If it doesn't offer to fill in, check the address">
A manager only fills your password in on the site it was saved for, so a copy of your bank's login page at a lookalike address gets nothing.[^ncsc-pm] When it doesn't offer, stop and read the address before you type anything in by hand. <Term slug="phishing">Phishing</Term> pages can copy how a site looks, but not where it is.

It's not always a scam. Some real sites use a different address for each country, and Bitwarden lets you add the extra one to the login so it fills there next time.[^bw-uri]
</Tip>

## Make sure you can get back in

<Tip id="plan-for-lockout" priority="optional" effort={10} title="Plan for forgetting the master password">
The NCSC suggests setting up emergency access through someone you trust.[^ncsc-pm] On Bitwarden, the person you pick can ask for access, and gets it once you approve or a waiting period runs out, but adding them needs the paid plan.[^bw-ea] With KeePass, your vault is a file, so keep a copy of it somewhere other than the device you use it on.

If you'd rather not keep the master password on a single sheet of paper, <App name="2of3 by Ente" /> turns it into three cards, and any two of them get it back. One card on its own is no use to anyone who finds it.[^2of3]
</Tip>

## Leaving LastPass

If you used <Swap product="lastpass">LastPass</Swap> before the end of 2022, moving to another manager isn't enough on its own. Bitwarden and ProtonPass can both import a LastPass export.[^bw-lastpass][^pp-import]

<Tip id="change-what-was-in-lastpass" priority="essential" effort={60} title="Change the passwords that were in your LastPass vault">
In 2022, attackers took a copy of LastPass customers' encrypted vaults.[^lastpass] Changing your LastPass master password now doesn't change that copy, and it can be attacked offline for as long as anyone likes. In 2025, US federal agents said in a court filing that crypto had been stolen using vaults cracked from it. Many of the people robbed had simpler master passwords, had been with LastPass the longest, and had kept a wallet's seed phrase in a secure note.[^krebs]

LastPass itself said to consider changing your saved passwords if your master password didn't meet its defaults, like the 12 character minimum it brought in in 2018.[^lastpass] We'd change them either way. Start with the accounts from [the email tip](#email-first), and treat anything else you kept in a secure note, like a card number or a seed phrase, as something a stranger might know.
</Tip>
