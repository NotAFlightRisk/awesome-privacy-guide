<Short>
Spend the first hour on four things: [a password manager](#password-manager), [two-factor on your email](#email-2fa), [automatic updates and a screen lock](#update-and-lock), and [one content blocker in your browser](#content-blocker). Then use the three questions below to decide what else is worth your time. You can [skip some popular advice](#what-you-can-skip), like changing your passwords every few months.
</Short>

## Why these four come first

Privacy advice usually comes as a long list where every tip looks equally important. They aren't. Google says most attempts to break into its users' accounts are automated, using passwords leaked from other sites. It also estimated that only about one in a million of its users faced the paid hacking groups it tracked, which charge to break into one particular person's account.[^g2019]

So the first two steps go after those automated attempts: a different password for every account, and a second step on the account that can reset all the others. Updates help protect your devices from malware,[^ncsc-updates] and a content blocker stops a lot of the tracking that follows you from site to site. When Google surveyed 231 security experts and 294 other people in 2015, 35% of the experts named installing updates as one of their top three habits, against 2% of everyone else. The experts were also three times as likely to use a password manager for at least some of their accounts.[^g2015]

## The first hour

<Tip id="password-manager" priority="essential" effort={20} title="Start using a password manager, and give your email its own password">
If you've used one password in several places, a leak from any one of those sites can let criminals into all of them. Your email matters most, because anyone who gets into it can reset the passwords on your other accounts.[^ncsc-email] A password manager makes a different password for every site and remembers them all for you, and the NCSC, the UK's cyber security agency, encourages people to use one.[^ncsc-email]

The [password manager guide](/guides/password-manager/) covers picking one from the list, moving your saved passwords in, and choosing a password for the manager itself that you won't forget. If that's more than you'll do today, the password saving built into your browser is fine on your own devices.[^ncsc-pm] Either way, give your email a new password that you don't use anywhere else. The NCSC suggests three random words.[^ncsc-tips]
</Tip>

<Tip id="email-2fa" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Turn on two-factor for your email, and save the backup codes">
With <Term slug="2fa">two-factor authentication</Term>, a password alone isn't enough to get in. Even a code sent by text helps a lot. When Google asked for one before letting a suspicious sign-in through, that alone stopped all of the automated attempts in its study and 96% of the mass phishing ones.[^g2019]

Before you finish, check that the recovery email and phone number on the account are ones you still use, and save the backup codes somewhere other than your phone, since they still work if you lose it.[^ncsc-2sv-guide] When Google challenged people in its study, 38% didn't have their phone to hand, and 34% couldn't remember their recovery email address.[^g2019] Then do your bank, and shops that have your card saved.[^ncsc-2sv] Never read a code out to someone who contacted you, or approve a sign-in you didn't start. The [two-factor guide](/guides/two-factor/) says which kind is safest and why.
</Tip>

<Tip id="update-and-lock" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Turn on automatic updates and a screen lock">
Updates fix security problems, and the NCSC calls installing them one of the most important (and quickest) things you can do. Turn on automatic updates, for your apps as well as your phone, so you don't have to remember.[^ncsc-updates] A screen lock helps protect the data on your phone if someone else gets hold of it.[^apple-passcode]

<Platforms>
<Platform name="ios">
For updates, open <Path>Settings --> General --> Software Update --> Automatic Updates</Path> and turn on Automatically Install. Your iPhone then installs them overnight while it's charging and on Wi-Fi, and tells you first.[^apple-update] For the lock, open <Path>Settings --> Face ID & Passcode</Path> (Touch ID & Passcode on older iPhones) and tap Turn Passcode On.[^apple-passcode]
</Platform>
<Platform name="android">
Most system updates and security patches install themselves. To check for one, open <Path>Settings --> System --> Software updates</Path>.[^android-update] For the lock, open <Path>Settings --> Security --> Screen lock</Path> and pick a password, which Google says is the most secure option, or a PIN of at least six digits. A PIN or password also means your backups are encrypted with your screen lock, which a swipe doesn't do.[^android-lock] The menus vary a bit between phone makers.
</Platform>
</Platforms>

Phones stop getting updates after a few years, and you can check online whether your model still gets them from its maker. The NCSC says to avoid using a phone that's no longer supported, though you don't need the newest or most expensive model.[^ncsc-updates]
</Tip>

<Tip id="content-blocker" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Add one content blocker to your browser">
Lots of websites load <Term slug="tracker">trackers</Term> from other companies, which log what you do there. A content blocker stops the ones on its lists from loading at all. <App name="uBlock Origin" /> blocks ads, trackers and malware sites, and works best in Firefox, on computers and Android.[^ubo] Chrome has switched off the full version, and Microsoft is doing the same in Edge by the end of 2026, which leaves uBlock Origin Lite, built for their newer rules, on both.[^mv2][^ubo] Lite is also the version there is for Safari.[^ubol] Brave blocks trackers and ads with its own Shields, so if you use Brave, you've already done this one.[^brave]

One is enough. An extension that can see all the websites you visit can read and change every page you open,[^cws] so each one you add is something else to trust. The [browser privacy guide](/guides/browser-privacy/) covers which browser to use, and the settings worth changing after this.
</Tip>

## Three questions for what to do next

What's worth doing after the first hour depends on you. EFF's guide to making a security plan asks six questions, and it's worth reading in full.[^eff] These three are where we'd start.

### Who would want your data, and how likely are they to try?

For most people it's companies and criminals. Companies use it for advertising, and <Term slug="data-broker">data brokers</Term> buy and sell it, sometimes after collecting it through ordinary apps.[^ftc] Criminals mostly want your accounts and your money, and at least against Google accounts, they mostly use automated tools rather than picking out one person.[^g2019] You keep companies at arm's length by choosing your browser, apps and services carefully, and limiting what they're allowed to see. You keep criminals out by locking your accounts down, which the [two-factor guide](/guides/two-factor/) and the [data breach guide](/guides/after-a-data-breach/) cover.

EFF suggests separating what could happen from what's likely to, and writing down which threats are too rare to worry about.[^eff] If you're worried about a government, the police or your employer, this page is too basic for you. EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense is written for that, and it starts from your own situation.[^eff]

### What would hurt most if it got out, or got lost?

For most people, their email account is near the top, since it can reset everything else.[^ncsc-email] After that it depends on you: your messages, where you go, your photos. EFF suggests listing what you want to protect, where it's kept and who can get at it.[^eff]

### How much hassle will you put up with?

Every step costs something, either time once or convenience every day, and EFF's plan has you weigh that against what you're protecting.[^eff] Pick the steps below that fit your answers, and leave the rest for now.

## The first month

<Tip id="location" priority="optional" effort={10} title="Check which apps can see where you are">
Where you go can show where you live, who you spend time with, which doctors you see and where you worship.[^ftc] In 2024, the US Federal Trade Commission accused a data broker called X-Mode of selling precise location data that could track people's visits to medical clinics, places of worship and domestic abuse shelters. Some of it came from other companies' apps that had X-Mode's code built in, and the FTC said those apps didn't properly tell people where their location was going. X-Mode agreed to a settlement that bans it from selling sensitive location data.[^ftc]

Go through your apps and ask whether each one needs your location at all, and whether it needs it when you're not using it. An app that shares where you are with family, or records your runs, might. Plenty of others don't need it at all. Turn precise location off for apps that don't need to know exactly where you are.

<Platforms>
<Platform name="ios">
Open <Path>Settings --> Privacy & Security --> Location Services</Path>, tap an app, and choose Never or While Using the App. Turn off Precise Location on the same screen for apps that don't need your exact location.[^apple-loc]
</Platform>
<Platform name="android">
Open <Path>Settings --> Location --> App location permissions</Path>, tap an app, and choose Allow only while using the app or Don't allow. Use precise location can be turned off on the same screen.[^android-loc]
</Platform>
</Platforms>
</Tip>

<Tip id="back-up" priority="optional" effort={30} title="Back up the things you couldn't replace">
Phones get lost, and laptops die. The NCSC's top tips include backing up your most important things, like photos and documents, to an external drive or a cloud service.[^ncsc-tips] Once it's set up, open a file or two from the backup, so you know it actually works.
</Tip>

<Tip id="one-big-switch" priority="optional" effort={60} title="Move one big service, starting with your email">
The <Swap product="gmail">Gmail</Swap> and <Swap product="whatsapp">WhatsApp</Swap> pages say what each one collects. Moving off one takes a while, because every account that uses your old email address needs changing, or the people you chat with need to come too, so pick one and finish it before starting another. Email is the usual place to start, since it's where every other account sends its password resets.[^ncsc-email] The [leave Gmail guide](/guides/leave-gmail/) keeps your old address working while you move, and the [WhatsApp to Signal guide](/guides/leave-whatsapp/) is about getting your friends to come with you. If it's your browser you want to change, the browser guide has a section on [moving off Chrome](/guides/browser-privacy/#leave-chrome).
</Tip>

<Tip id="aliases" priority="optional" effort={10} title="Give new accounts their own email address">
An <Term slug="email-alias">email alias</Term> forwards to your real inbox, so each site can have its own address. When one starts getting spam, you'll know which site it came from, and you can switch that alias off without touching anything else. A breach of one site then gives away that site's alias, rather than the address you use everywhere.[^ec-tips] These are the list's picks:

<Picks section="Mail Forwarding" level={4} />

Start with new sign-ups, since moving an old account means changing the email on it, which you can do next time you log in. The cost is that the alias service sees every email it forwards, so pick one you'd trust with your mail.
</Tip>

## After that

<Tip id="breach-alerts" priority="optional" effort={5} title="Get told when your email turns up in a breach">
<App name="Have I Been Pwned" /> will email you for free when your address turns up in a breach it finds out about.[^hibp-notify] It only holds a small part of what's been leaked, so no email from it doesn't mean nothing has.[^hibp-faq] When one does turn up, the [data breach guide](/guides/after-a-data-breach/) goes through what to do, in order. EFF suggests going back over your plan as your situation changes, so the three questions are worth asking again after a big change, like a new job.[^eff]
</Tip>

## What you can skip

Changing your passwords every few months is the big one. NIST, which writes the US government's rules on passwords, tells services not to make people do it.[^nist] The NCSC says forced changes do more harm than good, since people tend to pick a small variation of the old password, and stolen passwords are usually used straight away anyway. Change a password when it's leaked or you think someone else has it.[^ncsc-expiry]

Antivirus isn't where to start, either. Windows comes with Microsoft Defender Antivirus built in, protecting you from the moment you turn it on.[^ms-defender] In Google's 2015 survey, 42% of the non-experts put antivirus in their top three habits, against 7% of the experts. The experts didn't call it useless, but worried that it can give people a false sense of security.[^g2015]

Whether you need a VPN depends on a few particular jobs, and the [VPN guide](/guides/do-i-need-a-vpn/) helps you check. Do Not Track is worth leaving off, since Apple took it out of Safari because it could be used to fingerprint you.[^safari-dnt] Extensions that fake your browser's details often make you stand out more, too.[^arkenfox] The [browser privacy guide](/guides/browser-privacy/) explains both.
