<Short>
Passkeys and security keys are the safest kind of two-factor authentication. Codes from an authenticator app come next, and text messages are the weakest, though they're still far better than nothing. [Start with your email](#email-first), because anyone who gets into it can reset your other passwords. Then [lock your phone number with your network](#lock-your-number), [keep the backup codes somewhere safe](#backup-codes), and [never hand a code to someone who contacted you](#never-share-a-code).
</Short>

## Which kind of 2FA is safest?

<Term slug="2fa">Two-factor authentication</Term> means a stolen password isn't enough on its own. CISA, the US government's cyber security agency, ranks the different kinds by what can get past each one, and this is its order, weakest first.[^cisa]

Text message codes can be taken without anyone touching your phone. In a SIM swap, a criminal talks your mobile network into moving your number to a SIM they hold, and from then on your texts go to them.[^cisa] Cifas, a UK fraud prevention service, had nearly 3,000 reported to it in 2024, more than ten times as many as the year before.[^cifas] The NCSC still says a text code is a huge improvement on no second step at all.[^ncsc-2sv]

Plain "Is this you?" prompts on your phone are next. Attackers can keep sending them in the hope that you'll eventually accept one. In 2022, that's how someone got into Uber. They'd probably bought a contractor's password, kept trying to log in, and the contractor accepted one of the prompts in the end.[^uber]

Six-digit codes from an <Term slug="totp">authenticator app</Term> are made on your phone, so a SIM swap gets nothing. CISA puts prompts that make you type in a number from the login screen at the same level, since that's much harder to accept by accident.[^cisa] Microsoft Authenticator works that way for every prompt now.[^ms-number] Both can still be caught by a fake login page, though. If you type the code into a copy of the real site, the attacker can use it to log in as you before it runs out.[^cisa]

Passkeys and security keys are the strongest. A passkey is made for one particular site's account, and there's no code or secret for you to type, so a fake login page has nothing to collect. Apple and CISA both call them phishing-resistant.[^apple-passkeys][^cisa] A <Term slug="hardware-security-key">security key</Term> is a small device, like a USB stick or a tag, that you use when you sign in.[^apple-keys] On a Google Account with 2-step verification on, signing in with a passkey counts as both steps, so it doesn't ask you for a code as well.[^google-passkeys]

<Tip id="strongest-option" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Use the strongest kind each account offers">
It might be called 2-step verification, two-factor authentication or MFA in the account's security settings. Pick a passkey or a security key if it's offered, an app if not, and texts if that's the only choice. [2fa.directory](https://2fa.directory/) lists which sites support 2FA, so you can check before you go hunting.[^which]

When you add a passkey to a Google Account, your other ways of signing in and getting back in stay as they were.[^google-passkeys] Leave them alone until you've signed in with the passkey on each device you use.

After the first time, you're usually only asked for the second step on a new device or when you change something important, like your password.[^ncsc-2sv]
</Tip>

## When someone tries to get past it

<Tip id="never-share-a-code" priority="essential" effort={1} title="Never approve a login or hand over a code you didn't ask for">
A prompt you didn't start usually means someone has your password and is trying to log in. Say no, then change that password. Uber's attacker got in when the contractor finally accepted one.[^uber]

Scammers also ring people pretending to be their mobile network, get details out of them, and then pretend to be them to the network.[^vodafone] Whoever contacted you, don't read out or type in a code for them. Vodafone says a code meant for using online should never be given over the phone,[^vodafone] and Google says it never asks for a backup code anywhere except its own sign-in page.[^google-backup]
</Tip>

## Where to turn it on first

<Tip id="email-first" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Do your email first">
Criminals who get into your email can use it to reset the passwords on your other accounts.[^ncsc-2sv] It can be the way in to your phone number too. In one case Which? looked into, criminals got into a man's email, used it to reset the password on his Vodafone account, and then used that to move his number to another network.[^which]
</Tip>

<Tip id="then-the-rest" priority="essential" effort={30} title="Then do the accounts that would hurt most to lose">
The NCSC's list of important accounts is email, banking, social media and online shopping.[^ncsc-tips] After your email, this is the order we'd go in:

<Steps>
1. Your password manager, if you use one, since it holds your other passwords. The [password manager guide](/guides/password-manager/#lock-the-manager) covers it.
2. Your mobile network account, since it controls where your texts go (see [the next tip](#lock-your-number)).
3. Your bank.
4. The Apple, Google or Microsoft account that your phone or computer signs in to.
5. Shops that have your card saved.
6. Social media, where someone could message your friends as you.
</Steps>
</Tip>

## Keep your phone number yours

<Tip id="lock-your-number" priority="essential" effort={10} title="Lock your number with your mobile network">
While someone else has your number, your text codes go to them, and getting it back can take a while. One Vodafone customer waited ten days, and the scammers used the codes her bank and her email provider sent in the meantime.[^which]

<Region place="uk">
Give your mobile network's online account a strong password and turn on its own 2-step verification. Then ask the network whether it can add an extra PIN or password for big changes like a new SIM. Which? found that most SIM swaps are now done online rather than in shops or over the phone.[^which]
</Region>

<Region place="us">
FCC rules make every mobile provider offer a free lock that stops anyone changing your SIM, and tell you straight away when someone asks to move your number to another provider. The deadline for providers is mid-December 2026, so yours may not offer the lock yet. Ask, and turn it on once it's there.[^fcc-sim][^fcc-port]
</Region>
</Tip>

<Tip id="changing-number" priority="optional" effort={20} title="If you change your number, move your 2FA first">
Old numbers get given to new people. In the US, about 35 million numbers are disconnected every year and passed on, and when Princeton researchers took 200 recycled numbers for a week, 19 were still getting the old owners' sensitive messages, like login codes.[^recycled] Before you give a number up, switch each account to the new one, or better, onto an app.
</Tip>

<Tip id="text-recovery" priority="advanced" effort={15} title="Check whether a text can reset the account">
Some accounts let you reset the password with just a code texted to you, so whoever has your number gets past the password and the 2FA together. In the same Princeton study, two thirds of the 259 numbers they checked could have been used to take over someone's account at Amazon, Facebook, Google, PayPal or Yahoo through its account recovery.[^recycled]

Look at the recovery options on your most important accounts. If a text can get you back in and the account lets you remove your number, you can, but it's one fewer way back in for you too. Before you remove it, sign out and check that another way back in actually works. Security questions don't count, since the NCSC says they don't give the same protection.[^ncsc-2sv]
</Tip>

## Don't lock yourself out

<Tip id="backup-codes" priority="essential" effort={5} title="Keep the backup codes on paper, away from your phone">
When an account gives you backup codes, each one works once, and they get you in when your phone doesn't.[^ncsc-2sv] Google suggests printing its codes and keeping them wherever you keep your passport. Making a new set stops the old ones working, so throw the old printout away.[^google-backup]

Don't keep them in the same password manager as the password, or anyone who gets into the vault has both (the <Term slug="2fa">2FA glossary entry</Term> has more on that).
</Tip>

<Tip id="second-key" priority="optional" effort={10} title="If you use security keys, have a spare">
Apple won't let you turn on security keys for an Apple Account without at least two, and warns that losing all of them, along with your trusted devices, could lock you out for good.[^apple-keys] Where an account lets you add more than one key, add the spare too, and keep it somewhere other than your keyring.
</Tip>

## Picking an authenticator app

Pick one that can get your codes onto a new phone. If an app only keeps them on the phone you've got, losing that phone means using backup codes and setting up 2FA again on every account. These are the apps whose list entries mention backups or syncing between devices.

<Picks section="2-Factor Authentication" skip={['tofu', 'authenticator', 'authenticator-gnome', 'authenticator-cc', 'proton-authenticator']} />

<Tip id="leave-authy" priority="optional" effort={30} title="Leaving Authy means setting up each account again">
<Swap product="authy">Authy</Swap> can't export your codes, so moving to another app means setting up 2FA again on each account with the new app.[^reg-authy] Its desktop apps were due to stop working in March 2024, so plan to do this on your phone.[^reg-authy]

<Steps>
1. Sign in to an account, open its 2FA settings and set up an authenticator app again, this time with your new app. You might have to turn 2FA off and back on to do it.
2. Type in the code from the new app when the site asks, and save any new backup codes, since the old ones may stop working.
3. Sign out, sign back in with the new app's code, and only then delete that account from Authy.
</Steps>
</Tip>

<Tip id="leave-google-authenticator" priority="optional" effort={10} title="Leaving Google Authenticator">
<Swap product="google-authenticator">Google Authenticator</Swap> moves codes with QR codes. In the app, tap <Path>Menu --> Transfer codes --> Export codes</Path>, pick the accounts, and scan every QR code it shows (there may be more than one) with your new app's import option.[^google-auth] Ente Auth, for one, can import them.[^ente-import] A phone can't scan its own screen, so if both apps are on the same phone, check the new app's import help first. Those QR codes hold everything needed to make your codes, so don't take a screenshot of them.

Keep the old app until you've checked each code in the new one.[^ente-import] If your codes were synced to your Google Account, deleting one in the app deletes it from every device it was synced to.[^google-auth]
</Tip>
